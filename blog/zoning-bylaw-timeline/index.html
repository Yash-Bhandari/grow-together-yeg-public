Edmonton's New Zoning Bylaw: The Full Timeline

From City Plan to Provincial Order
Jacob Dawang

Eight years of engagement. Three council votes. A record year for new homes. And on October 22, 2026, one order from the Government of Alberta telling Edmonton to undo it by January 22.

Before the province rewrites the story, let's set the record straight on how Edmonton got its new zoning bylaw, and why it was never rushed.

TL;DR

  • Edmonton's zoning bylaw came out of eight years of engagement: 100+ events, 85,000+ visits to Engaged Edmonton, 7,500+ comments and a six-day public hearing.
  • At every public hearing, in 2023, 2025 and 2026, supporters made up roughly half of the speakers.
  • Council listened and adjusted. In 2025 it cut building length, widened side setbacks, limited side entrances and added design rules. In 2026 it lowered the height limit to 9.5 m and tightened lodging house rules. In practice, a new rowhouse today is about the same size as the skinny homes Edmonton has allowed for years, and about as tall as what you could build before 2024.
  • It's working. Edmonton permitted a record 17,500+ homes in 2025, and fiveplex-to-eightplex rowhomes are now the largest category of new homes. Only 0.6% of RS lots have been redeveloped with rowhouses.
  • The province has ordered Edmonton to roll it back by January 22. The city estimates that would mean 2,100 to 2,700 fewer homes in 2027 alone.
  • Email council and tell them to keep fighting the order. It takes two minutes.

Edmontonians asked for this

It started with The City Plan. Over two years of engagement, Edmontonians told the city what they wanted. In fall 2018 alone, drop-ins and pop-ups at malls across the city logged 1,200+ interactions, and young Edmontonians sent in 450+ postcards. People asked the city to "build up, not out," to stop sprawl onto farmland, and for "walkable communities with local shops." Affordable housing came up again and again. In 2019, more pop-ups and workshops tested the draft plan, and residents left 950 comments on its policies.

The City Plan turned that into hard targets. A cumulative 50% of new homes built through infill by a population of two million . Daily needs within a 15-minute walk, roll or transit trip.

Council adopted that vision 12-1 in December 2020. There was just one problem. Our zoning bylaw had 46 zones, and its last major rewrite was in 1961. To achieve the City Plan, Edmonton needed a new zoning bylaw.

The city listened. A lot.

The Zoning Bylaw Renewal Initiative launched in 2018, and the engagement never really stopped. Here's what it looked like:

  • 2018–2019: 15 pop-ups, eight workshops and an online survey reached 692 people, more than triple the city's target.
  • 2020: 12 discussion papers, from residential zones to signs. The city also held facilitated conversations with Black, Indigenous, racialized and other underrepresented Edmontonians about how zoning shapes their daily lives.
  • 2021–2022: Workshops testing the approach, sessions with Indigenous organizations, and students from Edmonton Public Schools sharing ideas on housing, open space and getting around.
  • Fall 2022: The first full draft. 11 workshops, 14 open houses, 13 pop-ups, 48,600+ views and 3,500+ comments.
  • 2023: A refined draft drew 18,297 visitors to Engaged Edmonton and 701 more comments, plus info sessions, one-on-one chats with planners and community league district meetings.

All told, the city counts 100+ dedicated engagement events, 85,000+ visits to Engaged Edmonton and 7,500+ comments, survey responses and feedback submissions between 2018 and 2023. If you didn't hear about it, it wasn't for lack of trying.

The first draft didn't cap homes at all

Here's something most people don't know: the 2022 draft set no limit on the number of homes per lot.

In the initial draft for the small-scale residential (RS) zone, the rules governed the building envelope, not the number of units inside. Height, setbacks, site coverage and at least 75 m² of land per home. A typical 50-by-150 lot could have held nine homes. A big lot, a dozen.

None of this was invented from scratch. It was borrowed from RF3, the small-scale infill zone already in place across many of Edmonton's mature neighbourhoods. RF3 had already dropped its absolute cap on homes for multi-unit housing. Instead, it set a minimum amount of land per home: 75 m². The new RS zone took that same approach, built on RF3's site coverage limits, and extended it to the whole city.

In fact, the old rules allowed more than most people realize. According to city administration, a typical 600 m² lot in the old RF1 single-detached zone could already hold six homes: a semi-detached house, two basement suites and two garden suites. Under RF3, that same lot could hold 12: four rowhouses, each with a basement suite and a garden suite. Today's RS zone caps it at eight.

The city said it plainly: zones "would no longer prescribe the exact number or type of dwellings allowed on a single property."

That's the right principle. If a building fits the size limits, why should it matter whether it holds four households or nine? A fourplex with big family suites and a nine-unit building with smaller ones can look exactly the same from the sidewalk.

By 2023, an eight-home cap on mid-block lots had been added to give "greater certainty" about what each lot could hold. It was a compromise. As we'd learn, even that compromise would be fought over again and again.

The City delayed the new zoning bylaw for more engagement

The new bylaw was supposed to take effect January 1, 2023. In April 2022, the city pushed it back a full year after community leagues and some residents asked for more time.

That year went into outreach: pamphlets in every 2022 tax notice, radio ads, open houses and direct work with community leagues. Then in May 2023, residents asked for more time again, so the city added two more months of feedback through July.

Council said yes after six days of hearings

The public hearing opened October 16, 2023. It was booked for three days and ran six. 293 Edmontonians spoke, split roughly in half: 134 for the bylaw and 159 against.

On October 23, council passed the bylaw 11-2 and on January 1, 2024, the new zoning bylaw came into force.

It worked

The results took a little time to show up. Projects take time to design and permit, so 2024 was a ramp-up year. Once builders got going, the homes came.

  • Edmonton permitted a record 17,500+ homes in 2025.
  • For the first time ever, more homes were permitted in five-to-eight-unit rowhouses than in single-family houses.
  • 30% of homes permitted in 2025 were within 800 m of transit, a new record for Edmonton.
  • Those 2025 rowhomes came from redeveloping just 0.39% of properties in mature neighbourhoods.

And the momentum hasn't stopped. In the first nine months of 2026, citywide permits fell 27%, but RS zone homes rose 17% to 2,107. Five-to-eight unit rowhomes are now the largest category of homes permitted in Edmonton. Since January 2024, mature neighbourhoods have averaged 397 new homes a month, about 2.7 times their 2009–2023 average.

New homes are landing where transit already runs. In the first nine months of 2026, 25% of homes were permitted within 800 m of an existing or under-construction LRT stop. Add homes within 400 m of ETS's frequent bus network, the routes that run every 15 minutes all day, seven days a week, and that share jumps to 41%.

City administration's own numbers back this up. Since 2020, a third of Edmonton's net new homes were approved in the redeveloping area. These are family homes, too: in a sample of 50 RS rowhouse permits from 2025, half the homes were bigger than 1,100 sqft and half had three or more bedrooms. Even without parking minimums, new RS homes still provide 0.48 parking stalls each on average.

Council kept listening and adjusting

Since then, council has adjusted the RS zone twice. In summer 2025, council held a five-day public hearing on cutting mid-block lots from eight homes to six. More than 165 people spoke, split roughly in half: about 45% wanted to keep eight and 55% wanted six. In the end, the motion failed 6-5.

The unit count made the headlines, but many people don't know council also reduced the size of new rowhouses. It reduced building depth (how far a building reaches into the lot), widened side setbacks (the distance from the side lot line), limited side entrances and added a host of design requirements to make new builds look nicer. Those changes alone make some projects impossible to build the same way today, like the Crestwood eightplexes, which by the way are a six-minute walk from the future Valley Line. They also mean a rowhouse built after mid-2025 isn't much bigger than the skinny homes Edmonton has allowed for years.

In February 2026, after an election that returned pro-housing councillors, another attempt to cut the cap failed 8-4. The 2026 committee meeting ran four days, with 125+ speakers split evenly: half for keeping eight homes, half for cutting to six. However, Council did vote to lower the height limit from 10.5 m to 9.5 m. The new height limit is only 0.6m higher than what was previously allowed in mature neighbourhoods, and 0.5m lower than what was allowed outside of mature neighbourhoods. This means that full 3 storey builds are not possible anymore.

City administration says the combined effect of the 2025 and 2026 amendments brings the size of new buildings close to what the old bylaw allowed. In other words, council had already responded to concerns about design and scale.

The province overrode it all

Then the province stepped in. On October 8, Premier Danielle Smith announced Alberta would force Edmonton back to its pre-2024 rules. The order published October 22 gives the city three months to restore the old density, uses and height limits in the RS zone. It also brings back the parking minimums Edmonton scrapped in 2020. After that, the city gets 18 months to write a new bylaw to the province's liking.

City administration has already put a price on it. For 2027 alone, it estimates 280 to 360 fewer rowhouse projects, 2,100 to 2,700 fewer homes and $420 to $610 million less local construction in the redeveloping area. Work on resilient landscaping, floodplain protection, wildfire resilience, a private tree bylaw and rowhouse design guidelines is now delayed.

Eight years of engagement, set to be undone in three months. Don't be fooled: this isn't about listening to Edmontonians. Edmontonians made this choice by electing a pro-infill Council in 2025 that has continually made adjustments to the bylaw to make infill work better.

The city just made progress on better design

On October 15, one week before the order, the city unveiled seven pre-approved infill designs by leading architectural firms. They range from a garden suite up to eightplexes for common 50-by-120, 50-by-140 and 33-foot lots. Instead of every builder starting from scratch, homeowners and small builders can start from a professionally designed set of plans. Drawing sets cost $5,000 to $18,000, and applications that use them get priority review.

It's part of a bigger push on design. Council's 2025 changes already required more windows and facade articulation on new rowhouses. Next up were rowhouse design guidelines in early 2027 and new landscaping rules, which the city says will improve streetscapes and help new homes fit in with their neighbourhoods.

Here's the irony: the province's order puts that work on hold. City administration says the rowhouse design guidelines, resilient landscaping rules and the private tree bylaw are all now delayed. If you care about how infill looks, the rollback makes things worse, not better.

What happens next, and how you can help

City administration has laid out a 90-day plan (dates subject to change):

  • Mid-November: Draft amendments to the RS zone are complete.
  • Late November: Limited, targeted engagement on the drafts.
  • December 10–15: Reports and bylaws finalized.
  • December 28 and January 4: The public hearing is advertised.
  • On or before January 22, 2027: Public hearing and council vote on the rollback.
  • Within 18 months: A full review to satisfy the province's infill policy, with more engagement than the rollback.

We've defended this bylaw before, and we'll do it again. Here's how you can help:

  1. Sign up for our newsletter so you know the moment the hearing is scheduled.
  2. Email council Tell them to keep fighting the order, because Edmonton's housing rules should be made by Edmontonians.
  3. Prepare to speak at the public hearing. In 2023, pro-housing speakers were half the speakers list. Let's do it again.
Tell administration you support more housing choice

Tell them you oppose the following:

❌ Cutting unit counts: Keep the limit at 8 units (don't drop it to 6).

❌ Increasing site area: Keep it at 75m² (don't increase to 90m²).

❌ Reducing height: Maintain the 10.5m limit; don't add "height impact" restrictions.

Here's a short example paragraph you can use as a response. It's best if you can write your own response, but the template response is still appreciated and very helpful

"I oppose the proposal to reduce the maximum number of dwellings on interior sites from 8 to 6, and I oppose increasing the minimum site area per dwelling. The RS zone has improved neighbourhoods across the city by providing more housing options, reinvigorating local businesses, and bringing back life in places with declining population. Furthermore, I oppose any reduction in building height or new restrictions on third storeys. We should be making it easier to build diverse housing types, not harder."
